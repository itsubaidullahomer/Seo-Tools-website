## What this words to time calculator does

This words to time calculator answers two questions that writers and speakers ask constantly: how long will this text take, and how many words do I need to fill a set amount of time? Paste a script or type a word count and you get the speaking time and the silent reading time side by side. Switch to the **Time to words** tab, enter minutes and seconds, and you get a word target instead.

Most calculators give one number from one hidden speed. Speaking and silent reading are different activities, and a toast, a 30-second ad and a blog post call for different paces. This page keeps them apart, shows where each speed comes from, lets you set your own pace, adds pause time, and times every paragraph of a script so you know when each one starts.

## How to use the calculator

**To find the time for a text (Words to time tab):**

1. Choose **Paste text** and paste your script, or choose **Word count** and type a number such as `1,500`. **Load sample** fills the box with a short example.
2. Pick a **Speaking pace**; the note under it says whether the figure is from a study, a standard or a rule of thumb. For a custom pace, use the **Words per minute** slider.
3. Pick a **Silent reading pace**. The default of 238 words per minute is the average for adults reading English non-fiction.
4. Optionally switch on **Add pause time** and set the seconds per pause. With pasted text, pauses fall between paragraphs (or between lines, if you prefer). With a typed word count, enter the number of pauses.
5. Read the tiles: **Speaking time**, **Silent reading**, **Words** and **Pauses**. The table shows the same text at every preset pace, and **Timing by paragraph** lists when each paragraph starts. **Copy result** and **Copy timings** send the numbers to your clipboard.

**To find the words for a time (Time to words tab):**

1. Enter minutes and seconds, or tap a quick target: a 15, 30 or 60-second voice-over, podcast intro, wedding toast, 5-minute speech or TED-style talk.
2. Read the words to speak at your pace (after pauses are taken out), the words to read silently, and the speaking range from 130 to 180 words per minute.
3. If you have text on the other tab, a banner shows how far over or under target it is.

## The formula, with worked examples

Everything on this page comes from one relationship between words, pace and time.

```
seconds = words ÷ words per minute × 60
words   = seconds ÷ 60 × words per minute
```

**Example 1: a script.** A 1,800-word talk has 12 paragraphs. At 150 words per minute the words alone take 1,800 ÷ 150 = 12 minutes. A 3-second pause between paragraphs adds 11 × 3 = 33 seconds, so the speaking time is 12 minutes 33 seconds. A silent reader at 238 words per minute needs 1,800 ÷ 238 × 60 ≈ 454 seconds, or 7 minutes 34 seconds, with no pauses.

**Example 2: a slot to fill.** You have 10 minutes and plan 15 pauses of 4 seconds. The pauses use 60 seconds, leaving 540 for speech: 540 ÷ 60 × 150 = 1,350 words, not the 1,500 you would get by ignoring pauses. Each 3-second pause costs about 7 or 8 words at that pace.

Times are rounded to the nearest second and word targets to the nearest whole word. Words are counted the same way as in the [word counter](/tools/word-counter): any run of characters between spaces, tabs or line breaks that contains a letter or a number is one word, so a stand-alone dash or symbol is not counted.

## Where the speeds come from

The silent reading figures come from a 2019 review and meta-analysis by Marc Brysbaert in the Journal of Memory and Language, which pooled 190 studies of adult reading. For English it reports an average of 238 words per minute for non-fiction and 260 for fiction, and 183 words per minute for reading aloud. These are averages across many readers and texts; individual readers vary widely, and a dense textbook chapter is read far more slowly than a novel, so treat 238 as a sensible middle, not a rule.

Speaking has no single authoritative number. About 150 words per minute is the figure quoted most often for conversational English, and comfortable presentations usually fall between 130 and 160. One industry reference is audiobook production: ACX plans finished audiobooks at 9,300 words per hour, which is 155 words per minute. The 183 figure measures people reading a page aloud under study conditions, not speaking to an audience, which is one reason it sits above a typical presentation pace.

The presets are labelled to match: study and standard figures name their source, and the slow, brisk, fast and very fast values are marked as rules of thumb. For anything that matters, time yourself. Read a paragraph of your script aloud with a stopwatch, count its words, and put that pace on the slider.

## Why some text reads and speaks slower

The word count treats every word alike, but words are not equally easy.

- **Technical and dense material.** Unfamiliar terms, long sentences and abbreviations slow readers and speakers. For legal, scientific or engineering text, choose the slow reading pace of 200 words per minute or lower.
- **Second-language audiences.** People reading or listening in a second language generally need more time. For a mixed or international audience, plan a slow speaking pace of 130 words per minute and keep sentences short.
- **Numbers, names and acronyms.** "2026" counts as one word but you say it as two ("twenty twenty-six"), and "$1.5 million" takes longer to say than its two written tokens suggest. Figure-heavy scripts run longer than their count implies.
- **Stories and familiar ideas.** Narrative and material you know well tend to move faster, so a story can pull you above your planned pace.
- **Delivery choices.** Emphasis, silences and audience reactions add time no word count can predict.

## Practical uses

**Toasts and short speeches.** Nerves push most people to speak faster than planned, yet an overrunning toast loses the room. Enter three minutes, read the range, and write to the lower half of it.

**Conference speakers and teachers.** Paste the script, add a pause per slide, and use **Timing by paragraph** to mark where each slide should appear. If the total is over your slot, the Time to words tab shows how many words to cut.

**Podcasters and video creators.** Convert a target runtime into a script length before writing, then paste the finished script to confirm, using the pace you actually record at.

**Copywriters and voice-over directors.** Check that a 30-second read fits before the script goes to talent, and leave room for music, product names that need clear delivery and natural breaths.

**Bloggers, editors and authors.** The silent reading time is the number behind an "X min read" label, though platforms use slightly different speeds. Authors can estimate audiobook length with the audiobook narration pace.

## Tips and common mistakes

- **Using the reading speed for a speech.** This is the most common error. A 1,000-word text reads silently in about 4 minutes 12 seconds but takes 6 minutes 40 seconds to say at 150 words per minute, nearly 60 percent longer.
- **Forgetting pauses.** Ten 3-second pauses cost 75 words in a 5-minute talk at 150 words per minute. Turn on pause time before you trust a tight slot.
- **Counting what you will not say.** Remove stage directions, speaker notes and slide titles before pasting. If text pasted from a PDF arrives chopped into short lines, the [remove line breaks](/tools/remove-line-breaks) tool tidies it so lines are not counted as paragraphs.
- **Speeding up to fit.** If a script is over time, cut content rather than talk faster. Above about 180 words per minute many listeners lose the thread.
- **Trusting an estimate over a rehearsal.** Do one timed run-through out loud, then aim to finish about 10 percent under the slot so a slow start does not cost you the ending.
- **Mixing up units.** For page length, use the [words to pages](/tools/words-to-pages) calculator; for limits set in characters, the [character counter](/tools/character-counter).

## Privacy and limitations

Everything happens in your browser and your text is never uploaded. Pasted text is kept only in this tab's session storage so a refresh does not lose your work, and it disappears when the tab closes. Your pace and pause settings are remembered in local storage on your device. **Clear** removes the text immediately.

Keep these limits in mind. The times are estimates built on averages, and real people vary a great deal. The speeds are for English; other languages have different average word lengths, so time a sample and use a custom pace. Chinese, Japanese, Thai and other scripts written without spaces cannot be timed by word count, and the tool warns you when a text looks like one. Paragraph timing assumes an even pace and cannot know where you will slow down for emphasis. Treat the result as a planning number and confirm it with a rehearsal.
